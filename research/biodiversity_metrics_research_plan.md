# Research plan: biodiversity metrics for a Brazilian Sustainable Sourcing Program

## 1. Purpose and decision to be supported

This plan defines the next phase of research needed to recommend a **prioritized, auditable set of biodiversity categories, indicators, and metrics** for a new Brazilian Sustainable Sourcing Program for regenerative agriculture. The intended output is not a single biodiversity score. It is a practical measurement architecture that:

1. prevents unacceptable harm and verifies legal/program safeguards;
2. measures changes in the pressures agriculture places on nature;
3. measures habitat/ecosystem condition and connectivity;
4. measures selected biological outcomes where feasible;
5. recognizes climate, water, soil, and livelihood co-benefits without double counting them as biodiversity outcomes; and
6. supports credible claims at farm, landscape, supply-shed, and program levels.

### Primary decision question

Which minimum and leadership biodiversity requirements and metrics should the program use, for which Brazilian commodities and biomes, at what spatial scale, and with what monitoring and assurance requirements?

### Phase boundary

This document is the **planning and source-triage phase**. It summarizes the supplied documents at a framework level and establishes the cross-walk and prioritization method. It deliberately does not make the final metric selection. All candidate metrics and interpretations must be verified against the exact clauses, definitions, applicability rules, and assurance protocols in the execution phase.

### Initial synthesis across the supplied library

The documents collectively point toward a **nested measurement system**, rather than one universal metric:

- **Safeguards and eligibility:** legality, traceability, no conversion/deforestation, protection of native vegetation and high-value areas, and respect for people and communities.
- **Pressure reduction:** pesticide risk, nutrient losses, water withdrawal, erosion/soil disturbance, and agricultural expansion.
- **State and condition:** extent, condition, restoration, and connectivity of native or semi-natural habitat, plus soil and freshwater condition.
- **Biological outcomes:** selected taxa or functional groups, invasive species, and threatened species/habitat outcomes, used selectively because they are costlier and more context-dependent.
- **Co-benefits:** carbon, water, soil, livelihoods, and rights, reported alongside biodiversity but not automatically presented as biodiversity gains.
- **Claims and assurance:** baseline, counterfactual/additionality where claims require it, leakage controls, persistence, monitoring frequency, traceability, and independent assurance.

### Important source-library gaps to address in execution

The current library is a strong starting point, but it is insufficient by itself for a Brazil-ready recommendation. The next phase should add and verify, at minimum:

- current Brazilian federal and state legal requirements relevant to native vegetation, water, pesticides, protected areas, Indigenous Peoples, traditional communities, and environmental registries;
- biome-specific ecological context for the Amazon, Cerrado, Atlantic Forest, Caatinga, Pantanal, and Pampa;
- current versions of relevant global and commodity standards and their Brazil interpretations;
- metric protocols and datasets, not only high-level frameworks;
- buyer, farmer, auditor, Indigenous/traditional-community, and technical-expert perspectives;
- feasibility and cost evidence for field, remote-sensing, and hybrid monitoring approaches.

## 3. Proposed program measurement architecture

### 3.1 Use four metric layers

Every recommended metric should be tagged to one layer. This prevents practice adoption from being mistaken for ecological outcome.

| Layer | Question answered | Typical examples | Program use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Safeguard / eligibility** | Has unacceptable harm been avoided and are prerequisites met? | No conversion after cutoff; legal compliance; traceability; protection of critical habitat | Entry requirement; pass/fail; cannot be offset by performance elsewhere |
| **B. Pressure / practice** | Are agricultural pressures on nature declining? | Pesticide toxic-load reduction; nutrient surplus; reduced soil disturbance; water-use efficiency | Annual management and continuous improvement |
| **C. State / condition** | Is ecosystem condition improving? | Native vegetation extent and condition; riparian condition; connectivity; soil condition | Core outcome monitoring at farm and landscape scales |
| **D. Biological response** | Are organisms or ecological functions responding? | Pollinator occupancy; bird functional diversity; soil-biota indicators; threatened-species response | Targeted validation and leadership claims, not necessarily every farm every year |

### 3.2 Apply metrics at explicit spatial scales

- **Field/plot:** management, soil condition, pesticide/nutrient pressure, selected field biodiversity.
- **Farm/property:** native vegetation, restoration, riparian areas, habitat condition, legal and program eligibility.
- **Landscape/supply shed:** connectivity, cumulative water pressure, priority habitats, leakage, and collective action.
- **Sourcing portfolio/program:** coverage, traceability, risk-weighted performance, equitable participation, and claims.

### 3.3 Separate three program tiers

1. **Minimum safeguards:** mandatory, pass/fail, no trade-offs.
2. **Core improvement metrics:** required baseline plus time-bound improvement trajectory.
3. **Leadership/outcome metrics:** stronger evidence and ecological outcomes used for advanced recognition or claims.

| C2 | Water co-benefits | Water efficiency, quality, access | withdrawal/tonne; water-quality trend; access safeguards | Field to watershed |
| C3 | Soil and productivity co-benefits | Soil health, yield stability, input efficiency | soil-health score; yield variance; input efficiency | Field, property |
| C4 | Livelihood and social co-benefits | Income resilience, labor, equity, inclusion | income stability; safe work; participation and benefit distribution | Household to program |

### Interpretation rules

- A **practice** (for example, cover cropping) is not itself a biodiversity outcome; map it to the pressure or condition it is expected to affect.
- A **co-benefit** should only be labeled a biodiversity metric when a defensible causal relationship and ecological interpretation are stated.
- Never aggregate a failed safeguard into a positive composite score.
- Prefer absolute change plus normalized intensity where both are decision-useful; intensity-only metrics can hide expansion or total harm.
- Require location and biome tags for all ecological results.

## 5. Cross-walk / mapping design

The cross-walk must operate at the **requirement or indicator level**, not just the category level. The companion file `certification_crosswalk_template.csv` provides the data-entry structure.

### 5.1 Unit of analysis

One row should represent one discrete requirement, criterion, indicator, metric, or guidance statement. Split compound requirements into separate rows when they address different outcomes or evidence types.

### 5.2 Required mapping dimensions

For each source requirement, capture:

1. source and exact version/date;
2. clause/page and verbatim-short requirement summary;
3. normative strength (`shall`, `should`, recommendation, contextual discussion);
4. applicability by actor, commodity, geography, farm size, and production system;
5. mapped taxonomy category and metric layer;
6. pressure–state–response relationship;
7. spatial scale and time horizon;
8. metric definition, unit, baseline, threshold, and direction of improvement;
9. data source, protocol, monitoring frequency, evidence, and assurance;
10. Brazil/biome relevance and legal relationship;
11. equity/rights safeguard and co-benefit links;
12. implementation cost, feasibility, and evidence confidence;
13. conflicts, gaps, and candidate program treatment.

### 5.3 Equivalence coding

Use the following mapping codes:

- **E0 — absent:** no relevant content.
- **E1 — thematic mention:** topic acknowledged but no operational requirement.
- **E2 — practice/process requirement:** action required, but outcome metric or threshold is absent.
- **E3 — measurable requirement:** metric/evidence is specified, but threshold, protocol, or assurance is incomplete.
- **E4 — comparable outcome requirement:** clear metric, protocol, threshold/trajectory, and assurance; suitable for direct program mapping.

Do not call two requirements “equivalent” merely because both mention biodiversity. Record whether one is narrower, stronger, geographically different, or only indirectly related.

### 5.4 Gap analysis outputs

The completed cross-walk should support four views:

- **Consensus view:** categories addressed strongly by most sources.
- **Differentiation view:** leadership metrics present in only the strongest sources.
- **Blind-spot view:** important Brazil/biome issues absent from the supplied sources.
- **Implementation view:** metrics that are scientifically desirable but currently costly or operationally weak.

## 6. Prioritization method

### 6.1 Gate before scoring

A candidate cannot be recommended as a core program metric unless it has:

- a clear decision use;
- an unambiguous definition and direction of improvement;
- a defined spatial and temporal scale;
- a credible data collection or estimation method;
- a feasible verification approach; and
- a statement of limitations and risks of misuse.

Mandatory legal and no-harm safeguards bypass weighted scoring and remain non-negotiable eligibility conditions.

### 6.2 Weighted multicriteria score

Score each candidate from **0 (poor/absent) to 5 (excellent)** on each criterion. Apply the proposed weights below, then conduct sensitivity analysis with stakeholders before final selection.

| Criterion | Weight | Core question |
|---|---:|---|
| Ecological relevance and causal validity | 20% | Does it credibly represent a material biodiversity pressure, condition, or response? |
| Brazil and biome materiality | 15% | Is it important in the target commodities, sourcing regions, and biomes? |
| Actionability for producers/program | 12% | Can participants change performance through identifiable actions? |
| Measurability, repeatability, and protocol maturity | 12% | Can it be measured consistently over time and across implementers? |
| Auditability and claims integrity | 10% | Can evidence and claims be independently verified and protected from gaming? |
| Feasibility and cost | 10% | Is the burden proportionate, including for smaller producers? |
| Standard/market alignment | 8% | Does it support interoperability with relevant standards and buyer expectations? |
| Sensitivity and timeliness | 5% | Will it detect meaningful change on a useful program timescale? |
| Equity, rights, and inclusion | 5% | Does it avoid harm and support fair participation and benefit distribution? |
| Co-benefit value without double counting | 3% | Does it add decision-useful co-benefit information with clear attribution? |

**Calculation:** `weighted score = sum(score_i / 5 × weight_i)` on a 0–100 scale.

### 6.3 Penalties and confidence

Report these separately rather than hiding them in the weighted score:

- evidence confidence: high / medium / low;
- risk of perverse incentive: high / medium / low;
- risk of double counting: high / medium / low;
- implementation burden by producer type;
- data availability by biome;
- dependence on proprietary tools or models.

### 6.4 Recommended selection logic

After scoring, classify candidates as:

- **Required safeguard** — pass/fail prerequisite;
- **Core universal** — required across program commodities/biomes;
- **Core conditional** — required when a defined materiality trigger is met;
- **Leadership** — suitable for advanced performance or outcome claims;
- **Research/pilot** — promising but not mature enough for program-wide use;
- **Exclude** — weak relevance, infeasible, duplicative, or high integrity risk.

## 7. Detailed execution plan for the next research phase

### Workstream 0 — Confirm scope and governance

**Questions**
- Which commodities, sourcing regions, biomes, producer types, and claims are in scope?
- Is the program intended for compliance, preferred sourcing, performance payments, public claims, biodiversity finance, or all of these?
- What are the intended baseline year, cutoff dates, reporting period, and assurance level?

**Activities**
1. Hold a decision-framing workshop.
2. Document program theory of change and claims hierarchy.
3. Establish an advisory group including Brazilian ecological, agronomic, legal, geospatial, social/rights, producer, buyer, and assurance expertise.
4. Establish conflict-of-interest and source-version-control procedures.

**Deliverables**
- approved scope memo;
- stakeholder and governance map;
- theory of change and claims register;
- research protocol and data dictionary.

**Decision gate:** no detailed metric scoring until commodities, geographies, claims, and user decisions are fixed.

### Workstream 1 — Build the authoritative Brazil context

**Activities**
1. Compile applicable federal and state legal requirements and clearly distinguish legal minimums from voluntary leadership.
2. Build a biome × commodity × pressure materiality matrix.
3. Identify priority biodiversity features, threatened ecosystems/species, freshwater systems, restoration priorities, and social/rights context.
4. Identify authoritative spatial datasets and their resolution, update frequency, licensing, and known limitations.
5. Document differences in feasibility and burden for producer sizes and tenure contexts.

**Deliverables**
- Brazil legal and policy cross-walk;
- biome/commodity materiality profiles;
- spatial dataset catalogue;
- legal-vs-voluntary decision rules.

**Quality control:** Brazilian legal and rights experts review all conclusions before use.

### Workstream 2 — Complete source review and certification cross-walk

**Activities**
1. Verify the current version and status of every supplied source and collect normative annexes, interpretations, audit guides, and metric protocols.
2. Add other relevant standards/frameworks selected through transparent inclusion criteria.
3. Enter every relevant requirement into the cross-walk template.
4. Dual-code at least 20% of rows; resolve disagreements and refine coding rules.
5. Produce consensus, differentiation, blind-spot, and implementation views.

**Deliverables**
- populated clause-level cross-walk;
- source summaries with exact citations;
- coding manual and quality-control log;
- gap analysis.

**Quality control:** target at least 90% agreement on taxonomy category, layer, and equivalence code in the dual-coded sample.

### Workstream 3 — Develop the longlist and metric specification sheets

For each candidate metric, prepare a one-to-two-page specification covering:

- name, category, layer, and decision use;
- ecological rationale and causal chain;
- exact numerator, denominator, unit, and direction of improvement;
- baseline, reference condition, threshold, and/or trajectory options;
- spatial scale, sampling frame, frequency, seasonality, and detection lag;
- protocol, data source, QA/QC, uncertainty, and missing-data rule;
- aggregation rules and prohibition on offsetting failed safeguards;
- applicability triggers and exclusions;
- estimated cost, skills, equipment, and producer burden;
- assurance approach and potential gaming/perverse incentives;
- Brazil/biome relevance, evidence confidence, and open questions;
- acceptable claim language.

**Deliverables**
- metric longlist;
- complete metric specification sheets;
- data and protocol catalogue.

### Workstream 4 — Stakeholder validation and prioritization

**Activities**
1. Conduct structured interviews or workshops with producers, local experts, Indigenous Peoples/traditional-community representatives where relevant, auditors, buyers, NGOs, and data providers.
2. Score candidates independently before deliberation to reduce group anchoring.
3. Review safeguards separately from scored improvement metrics.
4. Conduct weight sensitivity analysis and compare results by stakeholder group, commodity, and biome.
5. Document disagreements instead of averaging away material differences.

**Deliverables**
- completed scoring workbook;
- sensitivity and disagreement analysis;
- provisional minimum/core/leadership/pilot metric set.

### Workstream 5 — Feasibility assessment and pilot design

**Activities**
1. Test remote-sensing, farm-record, field-sampling, and hybrid data pathways.
2. Stratify pilot farms by biome, commodity, farm size, baseline condition, and data availability.
3. Estimate cost, time, sampling power, repeatability, and audit burden.
4. Test whether metrics detect expected differences and whether incentives create gaming risks.
5. Test landscape aggregation and leakage controls.

**Deliverables**
- pilot protocol and sampling design;
- cost and burden model;
- technical validation results;
- revised metric specifications and thresholds.

### Workstream 6 — Program architecture and final recommendation

**Activities**
1. Select required safeguards, core universal, core conditional, leadership, and pilot metrics.
2. Define baseline/cutoff rules, target trajectories, monitoring frequency, and escalation/remediation.
3. Define claims and assurance rules, including uncertainty, attribution, additionality, leakage, and persistence where relevant.
4. Create implementation guidance, reporting templates, and a phased adoption roadmap.

**Final report structure**
1. Executive recommendation and prioritized metric set.
2. Brazil biome/commodity materiality findings.
3. Certification and framework cross-walk.
4. Metric scorecards and sensitivity analysis.
5. Program measurement, reporting, and assurance design.
6. Pilot findings, cost, and feasibility.
7. Claims guidance and limitations.
8. Implementation roadmap and research agenda.

## 8. Research questions to answer explicitly

### Relevance and ecology
- Which pressures are most material for each commodity–biome combination?
- Which metrics are leading indicators versus lagging ecological outcomes?
- What reference condition is defensible, and at what scale?
- Which taxa or functional indicators are sufficiently sensitive, feasible, and interpretable?

### Measurement and data
- Which metrics can be measured with existing Brazilian datasets, and which need primary data?
- What minimum spatial resolution, frequency, and accuracy are fit for each decision?
- How will uncertainty, seasonality, natural variability, and missing data be handled?
- When can remote sensing support condition claims, and when is field verification essential?

### Program and claims integrity
- Which requirements must be pass/fail safeguards?
- How will no-conversion, leakage, restoration persistence, and reversals be handled?
- What claims can be made from practice, pressure, condition, and biological-response evidence?
- How will the program avoid double counting biodiversity, carbon, water, and soil outcomes?

### Feasibility and equity
- What is the cost and burden by producer type?
- How can the program avoid excluding smaller producers because of data requirements?
- How will rights, participation, traditional knowledge, grievance, and benefit sharing be governed?
- Which metrics require landscape collaboration rather than farm-only responsibility?

## 9. Quality, evidence, and reporting rules

1. Cite exact source version, clause/page, and access date for every mapped requirement.
2. Label findings as **document-confirmed**, **expert interpretation**, **empirical evidence**, or **research hypothesis**.
3. Distinguish legal requirement, certification requirement, buyer expectation, and proposed program rule.
4. Record contradictory evidence and minority stakeholder views.
5. Do not infer ecological outcome solely from practice adoption.
6. Do not use a composite score to compensate for conversion, rights violations, or other failed safeguards.
7. Report metric uncertainty and data limitations alongside results.
8. Version-control the cross-walk, metric specifications, scoring, and decision log.
9. Require Portuguese-capable review and accessible stakeholder materials for Brazilian engagement.
10. Reassess standards, laws, datasets, and thresholds on a defined update cycle.
