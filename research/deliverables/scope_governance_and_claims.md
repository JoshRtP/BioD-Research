## 2. Governance map and responsibilities

| Body | Required representation | Decision rights |
|---|---|---|
| Program steering committee | Program owner, procurement, sustainability, finance, legal | Scope, resources, thresholds, remediation, public claims |
| Brazilian technical advisory group | Biome ecologists, agronomists, freshwater expert, geospatial/data experts, legal and assurance experts | Methods, datasets, applicability triggers, pilot interpretation |
| Rights and participation panel | Indigenous Peoples/traditional-community representatives where relevant, worker/community groups, smallholder representatives | Rights safeguards, FPIC process, benefit sharing, grievance design |
| Assurance panel | Independent verification bodies and audit-method experts | Sampling, evidence sufficiency, conflicts, escalation |
| Producer/user group | Producers by size/biome, cooperatives, extension providers | Feasibility, burden, usability, unintended incentives |

**Controls:** publish conflicts; separate metric-provider sales interests from method approval; log every decision; require Portuguese materials; compensate community and producer participation; never average away safeguard objections.

## 3. Theory of change

If the program (1) excludes conversion, rights violations, and illegal/high-risk production; (2) gives producers actionable pressure and practice information; (3) supports protection/restoration at property and landscape scales; and (4) verifies habitat condition and selected biological responses, then agricultural pressure should decline and native/semi-natural habitat condition, connectivity, and biological function should improve. Climate, water, soil, and livelihood benefits are reported separately unless a direct biodiversity causal link is demonstrated.

### Causal chain and evidence boundary

`traceability + safeguards → avoided harm`
`management change → pressure reduction`
`pressure reduction + protection/restoration → condition change`
`condition change → biological response (subject to lag, weather, and landscape effects)`

A claim must stop at the highest layer actually measured. Practice adoption alone cannot support an outcome claim.

## 4. Claims register

| Claim type | Minimum evidence | Allowed wording example | Not allowed without more evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Eligibility | Traceability, legal/rights screen, verified no-conversion and protection safeguards | “Meets the program’s 2020 no-conversion and traceability requirements.” | “Deforestation free” if unknown-origin volume exists |
| Practice/pressure | Verified baseline, intervention, and repeated pressure metric | “Reduced modeled pesticide toxic load by X% from the verified baseline.” | “Increased biodiversity” |
| Condition | Consistent protocol, baseline, repeat measure, uncertainty, no safeguard failure | “Native-habitat condition index improved from X to Y in monitored areas.” | “Nature positive” |
| Biological response | Fit-for-purpose design, repeated surveys, appropriate comparator/attribution analysis | “Target pollinator occupancy increased in monitored fields.” | Causal attribution to the program without design support |
| Portfolio | Known coverage and denominator; no mixing compliant and unknown volume in the claim | “X% of directly sourced soy volume met program safeguards.” | Whole-portfolio claim from a sample without valid inference |
| Credit/finance | Separate validated method for additionality, leakage, permanence, reversal, rights and benefit sharing | Project/method-specific only | Using routine sourcing compliance as a biodiversity credit |
